# Photosynthesis (Biology Unit 4)

Photosynthesis is the process by which green plants, algae and some bacteria convert light energy into chemical energy stored in glucose. It takes place mainly in the leaves, inside organelles called chloroplasts. Chloroplasts contain a green pigment named chlorophyll, which absorbs red and blue light and reflects green light, which is why leaves look green.

The overall equation for photosynthesis is: six molecules of carbon dioxide plus six molecules of water, in the presence of light, produce one molecule of glucose and six molecules of oxygen. Oxygen is released as a by-product through tiny pores in the leaf called stomata.

Photosynthesis happens in two stages. The first stage is the light-dependent reactions, which occur in the thylakoid membranes of the chloroplast. Here, light energy splits water molecules, releasing oxygen and producing the energy carriers ATP and NADPH. The second stage is the Calvin cycle, which takes place in the stroma of the chloroplast. The Calvin cycle uses ATP and NADPH to fix carbon dioxide into glucose, and it does not require light directly.

Several factors affect the rate of photosynthesis. Increasing light intensity raises the rate until another factor becomes limiting. Carbon dioxide concentration also increases the rate up to a plateau. Temperature matters because the enzymes involved, such as RuBisCO, work best at moderate temperatures and are denatured when it is too hot. RuBisCO is the enzyme that captures carbon dioxide during the Calvin cycle.

Photosynthesis is essential for life on Earth. It produces the oxygen that most organisms breathe and forms the base of nearly all food chains. Plants store the glucose they make as starch, and they use it for respiration and to build cellulose for cell walls.
